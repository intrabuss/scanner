ORB vs DRB: Breakout Strategy Logic Explained
How the scanner decides what counts as a breakout

Our scanner runs two intraday breakout strategies side by side — ORB (Opening Range Breakout) and DRB (Daily Range Breakout). Both flag stocks breaking out of a key price level, but they define that level differently, confirm breaks differently, and are tuned for different moments in the trading day.

ORB Opening Range Breakout

The level: The high and low formed during the first 15 minutes of today's session (9:15–9:30 AM), captured fresh once per day right after the window closes.

The idea: The opening 15 minutes reflects where buyers and sellers first agree on value for the day. A decisive move beyond that range signals which side is in control for the session.

How a break is confirmed: Unlike a simple live-price check, ORB does not count a break the moment price ticks across the line. Instead:

  1. Price data is resampled into 3-minute candles.
  2. Every ~3 minutes, the scanner checks the most recently closed 3-minute candle (not the one still forming).
  3. A break only counts if that candle's close is beyond the opening range high or low.
This filters out brief "wick pokes" — a stock spiking across the level for a few seconds and snapping back doesn't get logged as a breakout.

Reversal handling: If a stock was confirmed broken and a later 3-minute candle closes back inside the range, that's logged as a distinct "reversed" event in the break log — not silently deleted. This keeps the full history of a stock's behavior visible.

Why 3-minute candles: It's a middle ground — fast enough to catch the move early in the session, slow enough to avoid reacting to 1-minute noise.

DRB Daily Range Breakout

The level: Yesterday's high and low (the full previous trading session's range) — not today's opening range.

The idea: Rather than reacting to today's first few minutes, DRB asks whether price is breaking into territory it didn't reach at all yesterday — a stronger, longer-horizon breakout signal.

How a break is confirmed: DRB uses a simpler, faster method than ORB — a live tick comparison, checked on every price poll:

broke_high = live_price > yesterday_high
broke_low  = live_price < yesterday_low

No candle-close confirmation — the moment price crosses yesterday's high/low on a live quote, it counts. This makes DRB more immediate but also more prone to noise than ORB's candle-confirmed approach.

The 15-minute "cool-down": A DRB breakout in the first 15 minutes after market open is deliberately ignored (downgraded to WATCH). Right at the open, price is often just reacting to an overnight gap — not a genuine intraday breakout — so the scanner waits until the gap has settled before trusting a DRB signal.

Shared Filters Applied to Both

A raw price breakout alone isn't treated as tradeable. Every signal — ORB or DRB — is passed through the same quality filters before it's shown as BUY or SELL:

FilterConditionEffect
Trend strengthADX(14) < 20Signal downgraded to WATCH (no clear trend)
Volume confirmationRVOL < 1.0Signal downgraded to WATCH (below-average volume)
Composite score7-factor weighted scoreMust score ≥65/100 for BUY, ≤35/100 for SELL

The 7-Factor Composite Score

Each stock's breakout direction is just one of seven inputs into a score (rescaled to 0–100):

  1. Price above/below EMA(9)
  2. Supertrend direction (Uptrend/Downtrend)
  3. MACD vs MACD signal line
  4. RSI(14) — above 55 bullish, below 45 bearish, neutral between
  5. Price above/below the day's pivot point (classic (H+L+C)/3 from yesterday's candle)
  6. Gap direction (today's open vs. yesterday's close)
  7. The breakout itself (ORB or DRB, depending on which scan is running)

This means a breakout on its own is never enough to trigger BUY/SELL — it has to align with trend, momentum, and volume at the same time.
